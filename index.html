28 of the 100 flags were human-written

A mid-size content desk runs every submission through an AI-text detector before an editor sees it. Volume is 8,000 documents a month and the detector flags 9 percent, 8,000 x 0.09 = 720 flags. Each flag gets a human second read: 12 minutes at a loaded $26 an hour, 0.2 x $26 = $5.20 per flag. The screening line costs 720 x $5.20 = $3,744 a month, and the desk treats that as the price of clean publishing.

Last quarter an editor audited 100 flagged documents at random. 28 were written by people. That is a 28 percent false-positive rate on the only number the desk never measured, and it prices out directly: 720 x 0.28 = 201.6 false flags a month, 201.6 x $5.20 = $1,048.32 a month spent re-reading work people actually wrote, $12,579.84 a year. The vendor quotes accuracy percentages; the desk was paying a four-figure annual bill to apologize to its own contributors.

The mirror number is worse. The team ran 300 known machine-written documents through one paraphrase pass and the detector caught 189, 189 / 300 = 63 percent recall. On a conservative 30 percent machine share of the same 8,000 documents, 2,400 pieces, the missed 37 percent walk straight through: 2,400 x 0.37 = 888 published with no flag at all. So the real monthly picture is $1,048.32 spent accusing humans and 888 machine pieces wearing a human byline. Neither number appears on the vendor's sales page.

The fix costs almost nothing because it is a measurement, not a purchase. Keep the detector as triage and audit 100 random flags every quarter: 100 x $5.20 = $520 per audit, $2,080 a year to keep the 28 percent measured instead of assumed. When the audit number moves, retune the threshold that quarter. A flag should open an investigation, and the paraphrase-drift gap should set expectations, never close one.

The detector arithmetic reads pastagi.com the way this desk should have, as eval guides that treat a score as a hypothesis until an audit prices it.
